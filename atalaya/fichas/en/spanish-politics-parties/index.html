How does Spain's political system work and who are the main parties?

Updated November 10, 2026 · General election: November 29, 2026 · Más en español

Spain is a parliamentary monarchy: King Felipe VI is the head of state, a two-chamber Parliament (the Cortes Generales) makes the laws, and a Government led by the Prime Minister runs the executive. Four national parties dominate: the centre-right People's Party (PP), the centre-left Socialist Party (PSOE), the nationalist right Vox and the left-wing Sumar, plus regional parties from Catalonia, the Basque Country, Galicia and other regions that matter in pacts. For the general election on November 29, 2026, the poll average puts the PP first at 31.5 %, ahead of the PSOE at 26.2 %.

1. How Spain's political system is structured

  • Form of State: a parliamentary monarchy under the 1978 Constitution. The King is head of state but does not govern.
  • Parliament (Cortes Generales): the Congress of Deputies (350 members) and the Senate. The Congress elects the Prime Minister and has the final say on most laws.
  • Government: led by the Prime Minister (officially the "President of the Government") with a Council of Ministers. See who is Spain's Prime Minister.
  • Territorial model: Spain is a unitary state with wide decentralisation: 17 autonomous communities and 2 autonomous cities (Ceuta and Melilla), each with its own parliament or assembly and government, and powers such as health and education.
  • Judiciary and Constitutional Court: independent courts, with the Constitutional Court ruling on whether laws comply with the Constitution.

3. How Spanish elections work

  • Voters pick a closed party list in their province; the 350 Congress seats are allocated across 52 constituencies with the D'Hondt rule, and a party needs 3 % of the valid votes in a constituency to win seats there.
  • No party has to win an outright majority: a candidate needs 176 votes in the first investiture vote, or more votes for than against in a second vote, to become Prime Minister.
  • In recent years governments have depended on pacts with regional parties.

Is Spain a federal country?

Not formally. Spain is a unitary state with a high degree of decentralisation: the 17 autonomous communities have their own governments, parliaments and powers over areas such as health and education.

350 seats. A party needs 176 for an absolute majority.
